\subsection{PCA and L2-normalization Ablation}
To validate the proposed architecture, an ablation study was performed to understand the importance of L2-normalization and PCA layers.
Table \ref{tab:pca_l2} shows that dropping \textit{PCA, and L2} is detrimental across every embedder, collapsing hit@1 to 1.4\% or 0.0\%.
This pipeline failure can be explained by the fact that without L2 normalization, FAISS inner product computes:

  $$\text{score}(q, d) = \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d} = \|\mathbf{q}\| \, \|\mathbf{d}\| \cos\theta$$

so the score is dominated by the vector norms $\|\mathbf{q}\| \, \|\mathbf{d}\|$, rather than the angular similarity $\cos\theta$ that actually reflects the structural similarity.
The result of this can be seen in Table \ref{tab:l2_similarity}.

This effect is worse for \textbf{GIN}, since its sum-pooling component means larger graphs produce larger-norm vectors, and removing the normalization, the retriever just ranks by graph size. \textbf{Combined} shows the same failure, since it inherits GIN's unnormalized scale in the concatenation. GIN explodes from 0.99 with L2 to 260{,}029 without. Whereas, Combined reaches 416{,}081

Therefore, L2 normalization is validated as part of the pipeline.

Analyzing the ranking score distribution per retriever reveals that retrieval difficulty varies significantly across Common Weakness Enumerations (CWEs), with each model demonstrating specific categorical strengths. Overall, the standalone CodeBERT-based retrievers outperform the Combined embedding model. This suggests that for these real-world scenarios, the underlying semantic signal carries more weight than the aggregated code structure representations.

\paragraph{CodeBERT Pattern Performance}
For the codebert\_pattern retriever, vulnerability classes such as CWE-190 and CWE-787 exhibit clear, distinct separations with no overlap between the interquartile ranges (IQRs) or whiskers of incorrect and correct matches. However, an anomaly occurs in CWE-20, where the ranking scores for incorrect retrievals are distinctly concentrated at higher values than the correct ones.

For CWE-476, while correct matches score slightly higher on average, their distributions noticeably overlap, lacking a clean separation. CWE-362 demonstrates a similarly fuzzy boundary: while the IQR for errors primarily resides in the lower half of the correct ranking score distribution, the 25th percentile (p25) of the correct retrievals aligns closely with the median of the incorrect ones.

CWE-416 presents a unique challenge; the incorrect retrievals exhibit a massive IQR, with its upper quartile significantly overlapping the correct score distribution. In practice, this wide variance means the retriever frequently ranks incorrect queries as highly as correct ones. Because the 75th percentile (p75) of the incorrect scores remains lower than the peak of the correct distribution but still bleeds into its range, establishing a reliable classification threshold is virtually impossible here.

\paragraph{CodeBERT Sequence Performance}
The codebert\_seq model exhibits a similar overarching trend but with notable deviations. For CWE-362, the incorrect retrievals have a wider IQR and a higher median compared to the pattern model. Additionally, the sequence model shows a broader spread for correct matches in CWE-416 and CWE-476. Generally, the sequence model tends to assign higher overall ranking scores across the board, which can inadvertently amplify the scores of false positives. Despite this, for both codebert\_pattern and codebert\_seq, the median ranking score for a correct match remains consistently higher than that of an error for the vast majority of categories.

\paragraph{Combined Model Performance}
In stark contrast, the `combined` retriever suffers from heavily compressed ranking scores. While codebert\_pattern and codebert\_seq successfully cluster correct matches in the 0.6 to 1.0 range, the combined approach condenses almost all scores—and shrinks their IQRs—into a narrow 0.2 to 0.5 band.

\paragraph{Vulnerability-Specific Challenges}
The standalone models perform exceptionally well for CWE-190 and CWE-787. In these categories, correct matches cluster tightly at high scores with narrow IQRs, while errors remain isolated as tight, low-scoring distributions. This suggests these specific vulnerabilities possess distinct lexical signatures that CodeBERT captures effectively.

Conversely, the models struggle significantly with other CWEs:

The CWE-362 (Race Conditions) IQR overlap between correct matches and errors indicates the models struggle to differentiate between safe and unsafe concurrent code operations.
Additionally, the codebert\_seq model exhibits a wide IQR for CWE-476 (NULL Pointer Dereference) errors, and its median sits dangerously close to the correct match median. This high volume of high-ranking false positives likely occurs because a vulnerable missing null check appears textually and sequentially identical to safe code where a null check is genuinely unnecessary.

\subsection{CVE level analysis}

CodeBERT Pattern achieves good performance in retrieving localized data-flow and bounds-check patterns. For example, CVE-2021-26788 ($0.93$) and CVE-2020-15211 ($0.73$) involve localized input validation and bounds checking (e.g., adding isxdigit() checks or guarding pointers within a single block). The good performance is explained by the match code, which is based on AST/CPG structural syntax. A good performance is also found when it is required to modify an existing AST node (e.g., reordering calls or tweaking conditionals as in CVE-2011-1768).

On the other hand, non-local semantic bugs have a low performance. For cases where the vulnerability stems from missing synchronization logic rather than existing control-flow nodes (CWE-362), we have the instances:
\begin{itemize}
    \item CVE-2020-12652 ($\text{MAP}=0.0$, $n=2$): A double-fetch race condition in the Linux kernel (\texttt{\_\_mptctl\_ioctl}).
    \item CVE-2021-32686 ($\text{MAP}=0.0$, $n=1$): A race condition between socket destroy and callback in PJSIP TLS connections.
\end{itemize}
These vulnerabilities depend on execution order and synchronization across functions, which AST/graph structural embeddings cannot capture in the current setting. A similar case happens with a Use-After-Free (CWE-416) CVE-2023-38703 ($\text{MAP}=0.0$, $n=2$), in which the issue is caused by a lack of synchronization between high-level SRTP transport and lower-level media transport.

Another fail case stems from the lack of access to external configuration flags and runtime state invariants, as seen in CVE-2020-10739 ($\text{MAP}=0.0$, $n=3$), in which a Null Pointer Exception in Istio/Envoy triggered under Telemetry v2 configuration.

Broad refactoring or multi-file architectural changes, such as locking macros or structural realignment in CVE-2020-35538 and CVE-2012-3552, also show a low performance(MAP<0.5).

Multi-file changes and configuration-dependent issues perform poorly across all the embedding methods explored. 

\texttt{CodeBERT\_seq} Graphic \ref{img:codebert_seq_cve_map}shows a better performance than CodeBERT\_patter on CVE-2012-3552, which is a race condition in Linux kernel IP socket option handling. It happens because CodeBERT\_pattern relies on regular expressions (\texttt{RE\_LOCK} and \textbf{RE\_UNLOCK}), to catch standard locking calls such as \texttt{mutex\_lock, spin\_lock, down\_read/write, and rtnl\_lock}. It fails when the code uses specific domain identifiers. 
This is the case of CVE-2012-3552 that uses socket‑layer locking \texttt{lock\_sock/release\_sock} methods, which are not included in the regex. Therefore, the regex group that signals a race condition remains empty. 
The remaining feature groups of the pattern embedding are unrelated vulnerability classes (Use-After-Free, Null Pointer Dereference, unchecked returns, array bounds, uninitialized variables, and alloc/free misallocations). As a result, the structural vector becomes near-constant noise. 
As CodeBERT\_Pattern concatenates the structural pattern vector with the CodeBERT embedding and applies the PCA, the semantic signal gets diluted, resulting  in a wrong retrieval for this embedder.  

Similarly, CVE-2012-3552, an issue in Linux kernel IP socket option handling, uses \texttt{compat\_mpt\_command} / \texttt{mptctl\_gettargetinfo} via internal ioctl mutex handling, consequently, without explicit lock/unlock calls. 
Because these domain-specific identifiers are absent from the vulnerability flow rules, lock\_nodes and unlock\_nodes remain empty for both vulnerable functions. 

On the other hand, semantic shows insufficient for cases like CVE-2016-3841 (Linux IPv6 options UAF), where structural embeddings capture the (missing cleanup node during option passing better than token sequences. As a consequence, the performance gets from ~0.33 (codebert\_seq) to ~0.67 (codebert\_pattern). Another example is CVE-2020-15211, in which structural embeddings isolate the AST sub-graph of the missing bounds check or double indexing pattern in the code of TensorFlow Lite array indexing. With that, the MAP goes from ~0.26 (codebert\_seq) to ~0.73 (codebert\_pattern).

\section{Generation Experiments}
To evaluate prompt sensitivity, two configurations of the agentic pipeline were compared. They share the same parameters: the model (gpt-4o), the retrieved example, the target code, and the dataset split. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{single\_turn}: few-shot marker prompt from the prompt registry (\texttt{single\_turn.py}).
    \item \texttt{tool\_calling}: ReAct prompt framework (\texttt{\_SYSTEM\_PROMPT}) combined with auxiliary reviewing code tools (\texttt{tool\_calling.py}).
\end{itemize}

% The \texttt{run\_meta.json} showed one fingerprint for both only because that field is computed at the run level from the "default" variant name, regardless of what the architecture actually does internally. So they differ in both prompt and control flow.

Comparing these two different fingerprints per record using surface metrics, \texttt{BLEU / ROUGE / Jaccard}, the analysis shows similar results across the two strategies.
However, this similarity is not a consequence of a similar patch quality.
Rather, this occurs because the metrics get saturated by the outputs dominated by the unchanged code body.

To have better insights, the evaluation will also consider the hunk metric.
When analyzing the patch difference of the patches generated by the \texttt{CodeBERT\_seq} retriever pipeline, for example, we can see that the fix regions differ.
Patch hunk metrics demonstrate that the \texttt{tool\_calling} agentic approach outperforms \texttt{single\_turn}, improving \texttt{hunk\_token\_jaccard} from $5\%$ to $15\%$ and increasing \texttt{hunk\_removed\_f1} by $+0.17$.
These results indicate that evaluating prompt variations and control-flow strategies requires patch-isolated metrics to clearly evaluate fix quality.

Similarly, applying the hunk metric evaluation to the ensemble retriever pipeline, we can notice that it indicates a high rate of wrong patches (\texttt{hunk\_added\_f1 $\thickapprox$ 0.02}). 
It occurs from the fact that the patches just reproduce the vulnerable input. 
Analyzing the pipeline a step earlier, the retrieval performance is poor with only a 29\% CWE-match rate. %, which suggests that it is important to have a good retrieval. 
Consequently, even though the agent workflow verifies the correctness of the retrieved CVE for the code snippet under analysis, downstream LLM patch generation cannot easily recover from initial retrieval errors, corroborating the importance of robust (context?) retrieval for effective automated repair.

Strong retrieval directly enables better downstream patch generation, but evaluating this success reveals a significant blind spot in standard evaluation metrics. 
Surface-level string and edit-distance metrics—such as BLEU-4, BERTScore F1, and Hunk F1—often create a false sense of repair quality.

Pipelines without retrieval, or those relying only on topological data, can achieve near-perfect lexical alignment indicated by Hunk F1 scores above 0.95 in C code, while only resolving 20\% of actual CVEs. 
They successfully reproduce exact syntactic surface edits but, as the LLM as a judge low metrics show, fail to resolve the root security vulnerability. 
In contrast, semantically grounded models may show slight drops in surface-level lexical precision, but they successfully resolve up to nearly 57\% of CVEs and 63\% of CWEs by generating functionally correct fixes.

\subsection*{Functional Validity and Execution Stability}

The trade-off between extends further. The sequential CodeBERT model generates the highest overall rate of functionally valid patches (36.0\%), closely followed by the Combined model pipeline.
Combined Topological features, on the other hand, produce syntactically stable code with the lowest runtime error rate just 4.0\%, yet they suffer from the highest invalid patch rate (64.0\%). That is, even though the code runs, it remains vulnerable.

Moreover, the effectiveness of specific embeddings varies heavily by bug type. Explicit data-flow paths in the pattern model lead to the highest valid patch rates for concurrency issues and race conditions (CWE-362), while the sequence model excels at localized control-flow fixes.

\subsection*{The C vs. C++ Language Gap}

C and C++ performance shows a gap regardless of the chosen embedding strategy.
Across all generation variants, Hunk F1, which measures localized edit precision, drops from approximately 0.93–0.95 in C down to 0.21–0.36 in C++. 
Semantic vector alignment suffers a similar degradation.

This performance drop is likely due to C++'s complex abstractions, object-oriented constructs, Resource Acquisition Is Initialization (RAII) patterns, and standard library templates, since they produce fragmented diff hunks compared to idiomatic, procedural C code, making automated patching more challenging. %TODO add reference showing that fragmented hunking task is harder
This is a structural limitation that needs further investigation.

\subsection{Patch Powered by DeepSeek-4-Flash}

Tables \ref{tab:patch_analysis_summary_deepseek} and \ref{tab:patch_analysis_summary_by_cwe_deepseek} show the patching performance of the DeepSeek-4-Flash model. As previously remarked, they reinforce the need for agentic multi-turn workflows for automated program repair. 

Single-turn generation fails to produce functionally valid code. Despite achieving token-based evaluation success rates of 28.1\% to 33.7\%, the single-turn LLM Judge Valid rates hover between 0.0\% and 1.1\%. On the other hand, the multi-turn Tool Calling pipeline powered by  DeepSeek shows higher functional validity. The \textit{CodeBERT Pattern} and \textit{CodeBERT Sequence} retrieval methods achieve valid rates of 27.0\% and 25.8\%, respectively, outperforming baseline Tool Calling without retrieval (21.3\%). This demonstrates that for pipelines powered by less powerful models, such as DeepSeek, enriching the context with the semantic and structural root causes of vulnerabilities via RAG can increase performance. Whereas relying on the model generation capacity may result in syntactically plausible edits being predicted. Nevertheless, it is notable that performance is lower than that of proprietary OpenAI models from previous subsections. Therefore, it reinforces the need for compensation in engineering effort when the pipeline uses open-weight models.  

% Additionally, DeepSeek exhibits a higher iteration overhead compared to Codex and GPT-4o. The model averages 5.2 to 5.5 turns per patch generation, suggesting a more exploratory and error-prone reasoning process before converging on a final solution. 

Furthermore, the \textit{Combined} retrieval strategy has the LLM Judge functional validity of 6.7\%. Repeating the behavior observed in GPT-4o, DeepSeek struggles to recover the vulnerability identification errors of the retriever, which composes with the errors of the model itself.

Vulnerability-specific patterns are also reproduced under DeepSeek experiment. As seen in Table \ref{tab:patch_analysis_summary_by_cwe_deepseek}, Tool Calling with Pattern retrieval achieves 73.3\% syntax success and a 93.3\% verify rate on CWE-787. Yet, high performance does not translate to LLM as a Judge's functional validity for out-of-bounds writes. DeepSeek's performance confirms the overall evaluation that reliable automated vulnerability patching demands agentic, iterative tool use coupled with isolated, targeted retrieval, even though further exploration on the agentic workflow is required to meet reliable performance for production code.
